# Scene Coherence

Use this guide when participants or objects share an event, interact, or appear across a sequence. Choose the relevant checks for the requested composition.

## One shared moment

Identify what has just happened, what is happening now, and what follows. Position each participant and object at a compatible point in that chain.

For a shared trigger such as a starting signal, a door opening, or an object being handed over, allow small natural differences in reaction and pose. Make the resulting states believable within the same elapsed time. Vary silhouettes and expressions according to each participant's role.

Distinguish simultaneous events from sequential interactions. During a pass, the thrower may be following through while the receiver prepares to catch. During a conversation, a speaker may gesture while a listener attends. Those different poses belong to the same moment.

For a sprint scene showing the first step after the gun, place the visible competitors in compatible early acceleration states. Show them driving away from their blocks, with slightly different stride phases and plausible separation along the track. Keep their progress and body lean consistent with the very short time since the start.

## Participants, attention, and intent

For each important figure, establish a role, action, position, target of attention, and any interaction. Include readable background figures in this reasoning.

Add supporting participants only when the setting or action calls for them. A competition can include opponents and officials. A solitary portrait follows the requested subject count. Keep the main subject visually prominent through scale, placement, contrast, and detail.

Preserve the approved composition when fixing a supporting participant. In a close-up, retain the protagonist's size, framing, and dynamic silhouette while repairing smaller figures in the available background space. Check the correction at the final viewing size so the main subject remains the clear focus. Follow the requested balance of attention for ensemble scenes.

Use gaze, head direction, torso orientation, and gestures to explain attention. A cook can watch the knife or pan, a catcher can track the ball, and a listener can attend to the speaker. Deliberately looking away should fit the requested emotion or story. Preserve the character's facial conventions when eye detail is simplified.

Background participants can have simpler faces and clothing. Their action, direction, timing, support, and shadow still need to agree with the scene. Review crowd groups at their readable scale and inspect individually legible participants.

## Hands, objects, and shared loads

Establish who supports each object, where the contact occurs, and where the load travels through the body or surface.

- Shape a grip around the relevant handle, edge, or surface. Align the wrist and fingers with that contact.
- In a handoff, choose the instant of shared support, release, or receipt. Give the exchanged object one readable location and consistent orientation.
- For joint lifting, show how each participant contributes support. Align their grips, load height, posture, and effort with the same object and movement.
- For tool use, align the grip, working end, target, and material response. Follow the chosen stage of the task.
- When an object rests on furniture or the ground, show plausible support, overlap, and contact shadow.
- For soft materials, place deformation at the relevant tension or compression points and carry it through connected fabric, straps, or cushions.

Use the anatomy and capabilities of the depicted subject. Adapt support and movement to animals, creatures, prosthetics, or fantastical designs while keeping the scene's chosen rules consistent.

## Space, scale, and environment

Establish the ground plane, relative distances, direction of travel, camera perspective, and key obstacles. Keep lanes, paths, tables, stairs, vehicles, and architecture aligned with that space.

Use overlap to clarify depth while keeping the defining contact or exchange visible. Frame the scene so the action can be understood without relying on hidden joints or objects. Keep hands, props, and nearby silhouettes legible where they meet.

Scale distant figures and equipment consistently with the viewpoint. In a start-line scene, depth across neighboring lanes and progress along the race are separate relationships. Show both correctly.

Judge progress from each participant's position relative to the shared starting plane, using the same perspective for lane boundaries and equipment. Match displacement to the elapsed time and local figure scale. Choose relative positions from the requested story or event evidence, and check that the composition communicates those positions clearly.

Coordinate environmental causes across the frame. A shared wind should affect loose fabric, leaves, and dust coherently. Water disturbances should follow the actual entry or stroke. Effects may persist after the cause, with position and intensity appropriate to their age.

## Apply the principles across scenes

| Scene | Shared timing and interaction | Useful physical details |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Race start | Competitors react to the same signal and occupy nearby stages of early acceleration. | Short travel from each start position, low drive posture, coherent foot contact and lane perspective. |
| Throwing and catching | Choose release, flight, or receipt, then coordinate the thrower, receiver, and object around that instant. | One object position, a plausible path, target-directed gaze, and appropriate hand readiness or compression. |
| Carrying furniture together | Everyone supports and moves the same load at the same height and moment. | Grips at usable edges, compatible steps, distributed effort, and a consistent object orientation. |
| Cooking or making something | Each person's gesture belongs to the current stage of a shared task. | Working hand and stabilizing hand, tool contact, ingredients or materials, and an appropriately supported container. |
| Writing, drawing, or studying | Coordinate attention with the page, screen, instrument, or demonstration. | Pen tip meets the page during a stroke, the book is supported, and the seated body rests convincingly. |
| Conversation or an emotional scene | Give gestures, gaze, and reactions a relationship to the same exchange. | Relaxed or tense posture, meaningful personal distance, and contact where people lean or sit. |
| Swimming, cycling, or animal movement | Select a phase suited to the activity and coordinate body, equipment, and surroundings. | Relevant water contact, pedal and handlebar alignment, or species-appropriate support and gait. |
| Fantasy action | Establish the requested fictional behavior and carry its consequences through the frame. | Consistent origins and targets of effects, reactions, displacement, and any light emitted by the effect. |
| Portrait or a still life | Follow the requested attention, mood, arrangement, and viewing angle. | Balanced posture or object support, readable shapes, and coherent illumination. |

These examples illustrate the reasoning to apply. Use the same questions for new activities that the user requests.

## Sequences and repeated characters

For comics, storyboards, or animation frames, choose a coherent moment within each panel and a plausible progression between panels. Track identity, costume, held objects, handedness when established, positions, entrances and exits, and the state of the environment.

Maintain a clear camera axis or give enough spatial information to explain an intentional change. Reuse approved character references across the sequence. Follow explicitly requested montage or symbolic arrangements.

## Review and correction

Read the full image as a scene before concentrating on attractive details. Trace each important action from its cause through the participants and objects to its visible consequence.

Inspect every readable background figure, then check contact points and lighting at a closer scale. In a race, compare the participants' progress along their lanes. In a handoff, inspect both hands and the shared object. In a conversation, compare gaze and body language.

When an error changes the meaning of the scene, correct it before final delivery. Identify the specific relationship to repair and preserve successful character and style choices. Review the whole frame after the edit, since a local correction can affect surrounding elements. Stop repeated attempts when they cease to improve the result and clearly report the remaining material issue.
